## Decisions
- Ratings 0-100 with current and potential per skill. Overall = weighted average of position skills.
- Hidden athletic traits (speed, strength, agility, explosion, intelligence) drive both skills and combine results, so the combine predicts ratings well but not perfectly. Traits are built from talent relative to the position (including star stretch), luck, and size, then rescaled to a spread of 15, so combine results stay within ~6% of real NFL averages and spreads (re-verified Oct 18 after a 10-17% drift from the scarcity tuning was found and fixed).
- Size trade-offs (Blake): players can be light or heavy for their own position, and it matters. `player.SIZE_TRADEOFF`: per spread of extra weight, +6 strength, -5 speed, -4 agility, -1 explosion. Heavy = better run defense/blocking/power; light = quicker pursuit/rush/coverage; overall roughly unchanged (a trade-off, not a penalty). Example DE: heavy run D 49 / pursuit 44 / 40 time 4.82; light run D 45 / pursuit 52 / 4.68. Shown quietly: gray italic "light for a DE" / "heavy for a DE" next to size, only for clearly light/heavy players (~16%).
- Hidden ratings scouts never see: interception avoidance (QB), fumble avoidance (ball carriers). Never shown on screens.
- Skill rating words and colors (Blake): Elite 90+ blue ("blue chip"), Very Good 80-89 green, Good 65-79 light green, Average 45-64 yellow, Bad 30-44 orange, Very Bad 15-29 brown, Atrocious <15 dark brown. Since Oct 21 these apply to the shown scale (median NFL player = 50; `engine/scale.py`), not the engine's internal numbers.
- Personality traits use neutral words (Extremely High ... Extremely Low), same cutoffs, because high isn't always good (volatility).
- Combine: 40, bench, vertical, broad, 3-cone, 20-yard shuttle, intelligence test, position drill. Averages, spreads and drill participation rates come from real NFL combine data 2010-2026 (nflverse dataset, drafted players); long snappers from 32 invitees 2006-2023 (rougher). Players skip drills at real rates ("Did not test").
- Positional scarcity (Blake: realistic at every position, QB the scarcest). `positions.TALENT_SHIFT` (overall talent level) and `positions.TALENT_SPREAD_MULT` (gap between best and worst; below 1.0 = replaceable) per position, tuned by search (re-run Oct 18 after the trait/size rework; talent star stretch raised 6 -> 9) to hit targets per league: QB 2 Elite / 6 rated 80+ / starters ~70 / best FA ~43; RB 1/6/72/55 (replaceable); FB 0/2/62/54; TE 1/4/68/47; WR 4/18/70/50; T 2/10/68/46; G 1/8/67/52; C 1/4/68/50; DE 2/10/70/46; DT 1-2/8/68/48; OLB 1-2/8/68/49; ILB 1/5/70/52 (replaceable); CB 2/10/70/47; S 1-2/9/68/50; K 4/14/76/65; P 3/12/74/65; LS 3/12/74/65. Targets are judgment calls (no clean public dataset of player quality by position); the table and reasoning live in positions.py. Result: 80+ counts, starter averages and best free agents on target (LS 80+ a bit under at ~10); elite (90+) tackles, edge rushers and corners a bit under (about 1 each per league), with Generational players adding more. League-wide mix: ~1.4% Elite, 7.8% Very Good, 21% Good, 64% Average, 5.5% Bad; average age 26.8. Applies to every player ever generated, including future draft classes.
- QB AI rule (future): treat QBs as precious (never release good QBs cheaply, price them high in free agency and trades) without making the "draft a QB over a good young starter" mistake.
- Generational players (Blake's idea; renamed from X-factor to avoid confusion with FOF8's X-factor): about 1 in 400 roster players, 3-7 per league. Hidden. Key skills top out at 90-99, high hidden consistency, steady development (low volatility). Max 1 Generational FB, K, P and LS each in the league at a time (Blake); `League.generational_allowed()` must be checked by future draft classes too. Planned: longer peak, fewer bad games, scouts/interviews may hint, and AI must value players by production so Generational players can't be exploited cheaply.

## Player development (design agreed Oct 19, build in Step 7) - a core reason Blake loves FOF8
- NOT Madden-style steady climbs to potential. The drama is in POTENTIAL shifting; current-rating growth is fairly consistent.
- Each training camp, every player's potential is re-checked. No fixed percentages (Blake): each player's odds come from his own factors, and league-wide rates emerge from them. Factors: volatility (biggest), age (young swing more and lean up; veterans less and lean down), position coaching, serious injuries (can permanently cut potential, per the design doc), Generational status (steady, almost never collapses). Tune so a typical season has ~88-90% of players barely moving (±0-3) and big swings rare; check actual rates after simming several seasons.
- Two layers of potential (Blake: a player can fall off one season, then return to his old level the next): (1) a long-term ceiling that drifts by small continuous amounts and occasionally takes a lasting jump or drop; (2) a season-to-season swing on top that fades gradually (part carries into next year, part wears off, with randomness in how much). Both layers move continuously every season; there is NO yes/no check deciding "permanent" vs "comes back" (Blake: must feel natural, not like a coded switch). Outcomes span a spectrum: full bounce-back, partial recovery, stays down, keeps sliding, occasionally comes back better. Age, playing time and coaching nudge the direction without guaranteeing it. Before locking numbers, sim several seasons and show Blake real player histories to judge whether it reads like a player's story.
- Normal aging decline is separate (position peak windows from the design doc, e.g. QB seasons 5-13, RB 3-9). Some shifts hit specific skills, not just the whole player.
- Coaches matter (Blake): a coach who specializes in the player's position tilts shifts upward and makes collapses less likely. Hooks into the staff system (design still open; Blake wants an alternative to FOF8's staff draft).
- If potential drops below current, current drops with it, roughly scaled (not necessarily point for point) - a real fall off a cliff (Blake: option A).
- Current ratings: each season move a steady share of the way toward potential, with small noise.
- When you find out (Blake): the shift happens at camp, but what you SEE updates in stages as the staff learns: some after training camp, MOST after the preseason games, smaller shifts during the season that often settle back near where the player started.
- Rust (Blake, FOF8 had it): players who haven't played lose a few points, regained over a few games of reps. Covers preseason and returns from injury.
- Screens will show the staff's EVALUATION of ratings, not true ratings (Blake agreed), so reveals can happen in stages. Ties into interview-based draft scouting. Today's screens show true ratings; switch when development/scouting are built.

## Game sim (Step 6, built Oct 19, 2026) - Blake: "make or break"; must feel like the modern NFL and never pre-determined
- Files: `engine/sim/` (rules only, no screens): `game.py` (downs, clock, possessions, scoring, halftime, OT, late-game clock management), `plays.py` (one play from the matchups on the field), `coach.py` (play calls, personnel, defense, 4th downs, two-point tries, tempo), `gameteam.py` (who's active and on the field, rotation, fatigue, form, reading ratings), `penalties.py`, `stats.py` (box score), `text.py` (play-by-play), `weather.py`, `norms.py`, `data.py` (real NFL tables), `tuning.py` (every knob). Screen: `screens/game_page.py` (Game tab). Test: `step6_season_test.py` (plays a 17-game season, prints league averages vs the NFL, standings, leaders; changes nothing).
- How a play works: every play is built from individual matchups (each pass rusher vs the man blocking him; each receiver vs the defender covering him; blockers vs defenders at the hole; the runner vs the second level and safeties). Matchups tilt real NFL outcome distributions (2023-2025 play-by-play, nflverse); randomness comes from those real distributions, so a better team usually wins the play but not always. Nothing is decided ahead of time.
- Ratings are read as z-scores against the league's average starter at that spot, with a soft cap (Z_CAP 2.3) so one player can't take over every snap. Each matchup adjustment is "centered" on its league-wide average (tuning.CENTER, measured over 1,500 games in 3 leagues) so an average matchup reproduces the real NFL baseline exactly. If ratings or knobs change a lot, the centers need re-measuring.
- Tilt math fix found during calibration: the first version multiplied big-play odds exponentially (a modest edge tripled 30-yard runs). Now tilts are in log-odds: an edge of m multiplies big-play odds by about e^m.
- Play calling from real data: run/pass by down, distance, field zone and score/time (logistic model) plus each team's lean; 4th-down go/punt/kick table from real neutral situations with late-game logic; two-point table; 2025 kickoff rules (touchback to the 35); 2025 regular-season OT (10 minutes, both teams possess, ties possible).
- Clock: real runoff between snaps (37.5 s after an in-bounds run), out-of-bounds rules (restart on the ready signal except the last 2:00 of the half and the last 5:00 of the game), two-minute warning, timeouts (late-game, end of half, plus occasional ones), kneel-downs that know how much time they can burn, spikes, Hail Marys, and clock-aware field goals (teams kick or burn a timeout instead of letting time expire in range).
- Penalties at real per-play rates by type (20+ types), accepted or declined by expected points; DPI is a spot foul called on incompletions.
- Rotation: backups get planned snaps (RB committee, WR3, DL rotation...), more when they're close in talent; starters stay in on goal-line/short-yardage snaps. Fatigue subs on top of that.
- Red zone: the field "squeezes" near the goal line for runs, completions and yards after catch, matched to real TD rates from each yard line.
- In-game injuries (placeholder until Step 7): some players return the same game, others miss 1-17 weeks (about 1 in 10 is long-term, often the rest of the season). The season test carries them week to week. Since Oct 19 (stat realism pass): about 30 injuries per team cost at least a game and teams lose about 100-110 player-games a season (real NFL 2024: about 95-250 per team). Ball carriers get hurt less per touch than before, and one random player per side (often a lineman) is in each pile, so injuries are spread across positions instead of piling on running backs. Quarterbacks get hurt less often than other players because the rules protect them.
- Calibration result (2,000 sim games vs real 2023-25, per team per game): points 22.7 (22.6), points sd 9.8 (9.9), average margin 11.1 (11.2), home win 56% (54%), plays 61.8 (62.4), comp% 64.3 (64.4), YPA 7.07 (7.02), TD rate 4.5% (4.4%), INT rate 2.3% (2.2%), sack rate 6.4% (6.5%), YPC 4.41 (4.48), 3rd down 39.0% (39.3%), turnovers 1.26 (1.24), penalties 5.8 for 52 yds (6.2 for 50), FGA 2.2 (2.0), drive results within 1-2 points of real shares. Player-season spreads match (QB comp% sd 3.1 vs 3.2, YPA sd 0.72 vs 0.78, sack% sd 2.5 vs 2.1, RB YPC sd 0.66 vs 0.57, WR catch% sd 6.5 vs 6.9). Leaders match (passing yards #1 ~4,770 vs 4,750; rushing #1 ~1,640 vs 1,695; receiving #1 ~1,800 vs 1,767; receptions #1 ~123 vs 130; sacks #1 19 vs 19; INT #1 7 vs 8; tackles #1 ~170 vs 150).
- Known gaps: drives per team ~5% high (11.5 vs 11.0); ties slightly more common than real (~0.3% vs 0.1%); 4th-down conversion 48% vs 54%; RB carry shares measured without injuries are top-heavy (64/31/5 vs real season 54/24/9; the season test with injuries spreads them more). Team strength spread at league start is narrower than the NFL's (true sd ~4.5 vs ~6 points per game) because starting rosters are fairly even, per Blake's parity decision; `league.ORG_STRENGTH` (default 0.0) is a dial that would widen it from day one (0.4 gave ~6).
- Revision after Blake's first season test (Oct 19): he saw two 2,000-yard receivers and leaderboards that felt "scaled" rather than random. My first leader check had been flawed (it compared pooled sim seasons in a way that understated the top of the leaderboard), so a new test plays full seasons (schedule + injuries) in several leagues, including Blake's own, and compares every leaderboard rank and threshold count to real 2021-24 seasons. Fixes: coordinator styles now persist all season (they had been re-rolled every game, which made every team look alike); defenses roll coverage to the most dangerous receiver or worst mismatch, more on passing downs, near the goal line and against a receiver who's hot that game; stars are targeted by design, so their volume comes with contested throws instead of extra efficiency; the ball spreads around once one man has been heavily fed in a game; diminishing returns on stacked edges in a single play (completion, YAC, runs); chips on star pass rushers; clean-up sacks spread sack credit; tackles weighted by tackling skill; running back usage varies by team (workhorse vs committee); red-zone targets lean to tight ends; QBs can be hurt on sacks and hits; injuries about doubled (about 22 players per team miss a game per season); pass/run balance and team pass volume matched to real (565 attempts per team, sd 49). Result over 12 sim seasons: no 2,000-yard receivers (max 1,879; real max 1,947); receiving yards by rank match real from #3 to #32 within ~3%; receptions, rushing by rank, carries, RB1 share (0.59, sd 0.12 vs 0.59/0.14), tackles and sacks match. Remaining gaps: fewer 4,000-yard passers than 2021-24 (about 5 vs 9; 2021-22 were higher-passing years than the 2023-25 calibration era), top rushing TD totals a bit low (about 16 vs 17), a few too many 10-TD receivers (9 vs 7).
- Stat realism pass (Oct 19, Blake: "a lot of the stats seem inflated, tackles included... needs to be as realistic as possible"). Method: every real player-season 2015-2025 (nflverse player stats incl. defense, kicking, punting; plus 2023-25 snap counts) was turned into "how common is each stat line" targets: leaderboard values at ranks #1/#3/#5/#10/#20/#30/#50 and counts of players past thresholds (e.g. 120+ tackles, 10+ sacks, 100+ catches, TE 1,000 yards, RB 60 catches), by position group, plus each team's stat split by rank (its top tackler, 2nd, 3rd...). The same measures are computed from 12-16 sim seasons in 4 leagues including Blake's. Main findings and fixes:
  - Tackles: real combined tackles = solo + all assisted credits (my first comparison undercounted real assists). Special teams tackles count in totals (NFL convention) and go mostly to backup LBs and DBs, sometimes offensive backups. Who makes the tackle now depends on how far the runner got (`tuning.TACKLE_POOL`): linemen near the line, linebackers on short and medium gains, safeties and corners downfield; assisted tackles are common in traffic and rare in the open field (real DBs make more solo tackles). Result: tackle leaders ~186 (real 183), position shares DL 21% / LB 35% / DB 41% (real 21/35/42).
  - Sacks: DTs had 41% of all sacks (real ~20%). Inside rushers now win less (`RUSH_INTERIOR`), clean-up sacks and second-man QB hits go to edge rushers and blitzers more than tackles, defensive line rotation is deeper, and safeties/slot corners blitz more. Shared sacks give no tackle for loss (NFL rule).
  - Pass defense: pass breakups were too rare and only went to DBs; now real volume (4.1 vs 4.3 per team game) with ~12% batted at the line by rushers. Some interceptions go to a different defender (tipped balls, overthrows to a safety).
  - Forced fumbles: every fumble used to credit a forced fumble; real is about 2 in 3. Strip sacks slightly rarer.

### Piece 4c: player development (built overnight Oct 20-21; numbers NOT locked)
- `engine/development.py`, run once a year at training camp (in the offseason, after the draft). Implements the Oct 19 design: two layers of potential (long-term ceiling that drifts every year with heavy-tailed randomness, so it now and then takes a bigger lasting step; and a season swing that partly carries over and partly fades, the carry share random 25-75%), no yes/no switch anywhere. Odds come from each player's volatility (biggest), age (young swing more), playing time (half the snaps or more leans up a little; under 10% leans down), serious injuries (8+ weeks can lower the ceiling for good; since Oct 21: 12+ weeks, 60% of the time), Generational (half the movement, no big drops). Each skill also wanders a little on its own. Aging: past his position's peak years (`PEAK_SEASONS`; QB 5-13 and RB 3-9 from the design doc as quoted in this log; RB set to 3-8; the rest are Claude's estimates and should be checked against the design doc's table, which Claude couldn't read here: no PDF reader installed. Current ratings close ~20-38% of the gap to potential each year (more when young), with a little noise; if potential falls below current, current drops 75% of the gap. Rookies' first camp just sets them up. Every player's overall/potential at each camp is kept (`rating_history`) and shown on the player card ("Ratings by season").

### Salary cap research: how the real cap grows, and cap room when free agency opens (Oct 24; Blake: "making sure that the cap in the game is realistic and scales up like in the real nfl over the years (i think it fluctuates depending on how the ratings for the league are which usually go up but research and make sure). NFL free agency is what makes the nfl so good with its cap room making teams play on a balanced playing field... i really want to make sure its perfect")
- **How the real cap is set:** players get a fixed share of league revenue, about 48% under the 2020 CBA (up to 48.8% through the "media kicker" as the new TV money arrived), minus benefits, divided by 32 teams. About 60-65% of revenue is national (mostly the TV deals, shared equally); the rest is local (tickets, sponsors). The TV deals are long (the current ones: 11 years, ~$110B, signed 2021, started 2023, league can opt out after the 2029 season; talks to redo them early started in 2026). So **TV ratings matter, but not year to year**: they set the price of the next TV deal, which then locks in steady growth for a decade. On-field quality (player ratings) plays no part. The only real drops: 2011 (new CBA, owners' share went up: -2.4%, then +0.5% and +2.0%) and 2021 (COVID, empty stadiums: -7.9%, with a negotiated floor and the loss spread over later years). The big jumps came with new TV money: 1998 +26%, 2006 +19% (new deals and a new CBA), 2022-24 +14% / +8% / +14% (new deals plus COVID money repaid).
- **Real growth 1995-2026:** 7.0% a year compounded (mean 7.4%), spread (1 standard deviation) 5.9%, from -7.9% to +26.4%; 5 of 31 years under +3%. 2015-2026: 7.0% a year, spread 5.2%. 2027: about $323-327M projected (Spotrac / OverTheCap, unofficial).
- **The game today** (`contracts.CAP_HISTORY_M`, `CAP_GROWTH`, `set_cap`): every real cap 1994-2026 is correct (2026 = $301.2M, checked against the NFL's announcement). After 2026, each year is an independent random draw: 7.0% average (right), but spread only 2.4% (real 5-6%), never below +2% or above +13%, never a drop, no TV-deal eras. 200 test leagues: the cap passes $1B around 2045 (median $1.17B in 2046; real history grew 8.7x in 32 years, so that's in line). Minimum salaries follow the 2020 CBA table to 2030, then scale with the cap (real minimums run about 0.29-0.33% of the cap, so scaling is a fair stand-in for future CBAs).
- **Cap room the day free agency opens** (scratchpad `fa_room.py`, the 5 offseasons of the integration run, AI teams, top-51 rule; real from OverTheCap via CBS Sports, March 2024 and March 2025, teams over the cap counted as ~0 since they must get under by the start of the league year): most room 32-70% of the cap (real 40%, 45%); median team **15-19% and rising each year** (real ~14% both years); lowest tenth of teams **~9%** (real ~0); teams with under 2% of the cap: **0 of 31** (real 7-8 of 32). So the game's AI teams are too even and too careful: no one arrives in "cap hell", which in the real NFL forces veterans to be cut into the market (cap casualties) and keeps contenders from buying everyone. Dead money is also on the low side (contracts session: 4.5-5.2% vs real 5-10%), the same pattern.
- **Blake's decisions (Oct 24):** (1) **"TV-deal eras"** for the cap after 2026; (2) each deal's size: **"mostly market, some buzz"** (his "ratings" idea, kept to the size it has in real life); (3) AI teams' cap room: **"Yes, match the real NFL"** (contenders go all in, some arrive at free agency with no room and must cut veterans; rebuilders sit on room).
- **Built: `engine/league_money.py`** (rules; `contracts.set_cap` asks it for each league year after 2026). A normal year grows 6.8% +/-1.3% (real between-deal years). TV deals: 7-11 seasons, signed 1-2 league years ahead (the real current deals end after 2029, so the first in-game deal starts in 2030); the new money adds extra growth of 9 +/-7 points (from -4 to +30), 70% in its first season and 30% in its second; buzz moves it by 4 points per point of buzz averaged over the seasons since the last deal (a typical average spreads about +/-0.5, so buzz is worth about +/-2 points against the market's +/-7). Labor deals every 10 years (the real one ends after 2030): owners win 35% (growth 8, 5 and 3 points lower for three years, like 2011-13), smooth 45%, players win 20% (+5 points in the first year). Shocks: 2% a year, announced the season before, the cap falls 4-9% and the lost growth comes back 50% / 30% over the next two seasons. **Next year's cap is fixed a year ahead** (`league.tv["proj"]`; the real NFL tells teams in December) and lands within about 0.6% of it; AI planning (`cap_for` of a future season) uses the projection, then the expected pace with every signed deal.
